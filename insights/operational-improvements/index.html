Small Operational Improvements That Can Make a Big Difference

Sometimes the best opportunities to improve a business are already inside it.

When business owners think about improving profitability, the first ideas are often increasing sales, raising prices, or reducing expenses. Those are all worth considering. But I've found that some of the most useful improvements come from looking at how the business actually operates or could operate better.

Information gets entered more than once. Reports take too long to prepare. Employees spend time bouncing between systems. Important decisions are made using spreadsheets that may not agree with the accounting records.

Individually, these problems may seem minor, but collectively, they can consume time, create unnecessary costs, and increase risk.

Look at the Process, Not Just the Expense

A business may recognize that its accounting department is spending too much time preparing monthly reports – the immediate reaction might be to hire another employee.

But before doing that, I'd want to understand why the process takes so long.

Is information being collected manually? Are different departments maintaining separate records? Does someone have to reconcile the same data every month? Are reports being created that nobody actually uses?

Sometimes the solution is additional staffing. Other times it's improving the process or making better use of the systems already in place.

The important thing is understanding the cause before deciding on the solution.

Better Information Leads to Better Decisions

Operational improvements aren't limited to reducing costs.

Reliable information, available when it's needed, can make a significant difference in how a business is managed. That might mean developing a dashboard showing sales, gross margins, receivables, cash, or other key performance indicators. It might involve integrating information from accounting, sales, and operating systems so management doesn't have to assemble reports manually.

Or it may be as straightforward as establishing a consistent monthly reporting process.

I've found that the goal isn't necessarily more information. It's getting the right information to the right people in time for them to use it.

Technology Should Solve a Problem

AI and automation are creating opportunities to improve processes that previously required considerable manual effort.

For example, automation may help collect information from different systems, identify unusual transactions, prepare recurring reports, or reduce repetitive data entry.

But I wouldn't recommend implementing technology simply because it's new.

First, understand the process and what needs improvement. Then determine whether automation, AI, or a better use of existing software can help.

It's also important to consider accuracy, data security, and appropriate human review. Automating a poorly designed process doesn't necessarily make it a good process.

Don't Overlook Risk

Operational efficiency and risk management often go together.

Weak approval procedures, inadequate separation of duties, inconsistent reconciliations, or too much reliance on one employee can create problems that aren't immediately visible in the financial statements.

Improving these controls doesn't have to mean creating unnecessary bureaucracy. It means putting reasonable safeguards in place to protect the business while allowing employees to work efficiently.

Start with Something Manageable

Not every improvement requires a major system implementation or a large consulting project. Sometimes a better reporting process, a simple automation, or a change in responsibilities can produce meaningful results.

I generally prefer to identify a few improvements that are practical, measurable, and achievable, then build from there.

The objective isn't to make the business more complicated. It's to make it easier to operate, easier to manage, and better prepared for whatever comes next.
