Profitable Growth: It's Not Just About More Revenue

Growing sales is important, but understanding what that growth contributes to the business is just as important.

Most business owners want to grow. More customers, higher sales, new markets, and additional products or services are usually viewed as signs that things are moving in the right direction… and often they are.

But I've learned that more revenue doesn't always mean a stronger business.

Growth can create new expenses, increase working capital requirements, strain existing systems, and place additional demands on employees. Sometimes a business grows significantly without improving its profitability or cash position.

That's why I think it's important to look beyond the top line.

Understand Where the Profit Comes From

Not all revenue contributes equally to profitability.

Some products or services have attractive margins but require significant support. Some customers generate substantial sales but demand discounts, extended payment terms, or additional attention from employees.

A business may have a large customer that appears valuable based on revenue alone but contributes relatively little after the associated costs are considered.

Understanding profitability by product, service, customer, or business segment can reveal some interesting things.

For example, would the business be better off increasing sales of an existing profitable product rather than introducing something new? Are certain customers consuming resources that could be used more effectively elsewhere?

These aren't always easy questions to answer, particularly when accounting systems don't capture costs at the level management needs.

But the analysis can change how a business approaches growth.

Growth Requires Cash

Even profitable growth can create cash flow problems.

Consider a distributor that receives a large order from a new customer. It may need to purchase inventory, pay freight, and cover payroll well before collecting payment.

The sale could be profitable, but the company still needs enough cash to finance it.

Hiring additional employees, expanding facilities, and investing in equipment create similar challenges.

This is where financial forecasts and scenario models become useful.

Before committing to an expansion, I like to understand the expected profitability, the cash required, and how long it may take for the investment to generate a return.

And I want to know what happens if sales take longer to develop than anticipated.

Measure What Matters

Revenue and net income are important, but they don't tell the entire story.

Depending on the business, useful measures might include gross margin by product, customer profitability, labor utilization, recurring revenue, customer retention, inventory turnover, or cash conversion.

The objective isn't to create a dashboard filled with dozens of metrics.

It's to identify a manageable number of key performance indicators (KPIs) that help management understand what's working, what isn't, and where attention is needed.

I've found that a few meaningful measures, reviewed consistently, are more useful than an elaborate report nobody uses.

Build a Stronger Business, Not Just a Bigger One

Growth should improve the business, not simply make it more complicated.

Sometimes the best opportunity is increasing sales. Other times it's improving pricing, reducing unnecessary costs, making better use of existing resources, or concentrating on more profitable customers.

Before pursuing growth, it's worth understanding what that growth will require and what it should contribute.

The goal isn't necessarily to build the largest business possible. It's to build one that's financially sound, operationally effective, and capable of supporting its future.
